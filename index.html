Can you trust what you see?

Optical illusions show how context can change our perception.

Seeing is more than recording an image. Our visual system interprets shapes, brightness, and depth using the information around them. Sometimes, what we perceive differs from what is actually on the page.

Look closely

1. Ebbinghaus illusion

Which orange circle looks larger?

What is happening? Both orange circles are the same size. The one among smaller circles often looks larger because we judge size in relation to nearby objects.

The surrounding context changes; the center circles do not.

2. Ponzo illusion

Which blue line looks longer?

What is happening? The blue lines have equal lengths. The converging lines suggest depth, like railway tracks. One explanation is that the upper line seems farther away, so the visual system interprets it as larger.

Depth cues can influence our judgment of size.

Try it yourself

Change the context and see what happens. The strength of an illusion can vary from person to person.

3. Müller-Lyer illusion

Compare the two horizontal lines, then remove the fins to check your impression.

Fins are visible. Do the lines look equal?

Why it works: The angled ends affect our judgment of the central lines. Researchers have proposed several explanations, including depth cues and the influence of the overall shape. Removing the fins makes the equal lengths easier to see.

4. Simultaneous brightness contrast

Are the two small squares the same gray? Move the slider to change their backgrounds.

At 0%, both backgrounds match. Only the backgrounds change.

Why it works: Brightness is perceived relative to the surroundings. A gray square on a dark background can look lighter than the same square on a light background. Both small squares always use exactly the same color: #808080.
